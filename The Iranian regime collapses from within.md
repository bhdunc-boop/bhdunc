The Iranian regime collapses from within during the war, not after. The standard analyst assumption is that external attack rallies a population. But the IRGC is hated by huge chunks of Iranian society, and a sustained loss of life from American bombing combined with economic pain could trigger a 1979-in-reverse moment where the urban middle class, parts of the military, and minority regions simultaneously break. The Shah fell faster than anyone predicted; regimes can also fall faster than anyone predicts. Imagine: by mid-2026, a coalition of reformist clerics, IRGC defectors, and protest leaders announces a transitional government from Tabriz. The U.S. doesn't get the satisfaction of "winning" because it's clearly an Iranian act, but it also doesn't get to install a puppet. A genuinely independent post-theocratic Iran would be one of the most disruptive geopolitical events in 50 years and almost no one has it modeled.

Saudi Arabia switches sides, openly. MBS has been hedging publicly for years. If the war drags out, oil installations get hit, and the U.S. seems to be losing, Saudi Arabia's logic for staying in the American camp evaporates. Picture: an OPEC+ announcement quietly settling oil sales in yuan and rubles, a Riyadh-Tehran detente brokered by Beijing (this almost happened in 2023), and the Saudis essentially leaving the dollar system not by collapsing it but by walking out. This isn't a curveball anymore — it's a 25-30% scenario.

A nuclear incident. Everyone watches Iran's enrichment program. The actual nuclear flash point is more likely to be Pakistan, where political instability, an Islamic-solidarity political movement triggered by the war, and a degraded military command structure could lead to a real nuclear security crisis. Not necessarily a use, but a transfer or loss-of-control event that reshapes everything. Pakistan-as-the-real-nuclear-story is a recurring theme in serious think-tank red-teaming and almost never makes it into public discourse.

Climate intervention by Russia. Not war — geoengineering. Russia has the worst demographic outlook of any major power, an economy that depends on fossil fuels nobody wants in 30 years, and a strategic interest in a warmer Arctic. There's been quiet discussion in Russian scientific circles about unilateral solar geoengineering. If the world fragments into hostile blocs and climate cooperation collapses, somebody, eventually, deploys stratospheric aerosols unilaterally. Russia is the single most likely candidate. The geopolitics of "you've changed my country's monsoon pattern" is a genre of conflict we haven't experienced yet.

The AI capability discontinuity that makes all this irrelevant. But if any of the leading labs hit something like a genuine inference breakthrough in the next 18 months — meaningfully autonomous agents, scientific R&D acceleration, or an actual military application that breaks the offense-defense balance — the geopolitical board gets reshuffled in ways that make 1930s-style empire competition look quaint. The U.S. might "win" the empire game and find that empires don't matter anymore. Or China might leap ahead in a domain that isn't shipping lanes. The probability of an AI capability moment that's geopolitically discontinuous in this window: I'd say 20-30%, and it's the single biggest unknown in the whole picture.

A pandemic that isn't COVID-shaped. H5N1 is the obvious candidate, but the curveball is something we're not watching — a fungal pathogen taking advantage of warming, an antibiotic-resistant bacterial outbreak, or a CRISPR-derived accident. Wartime conditions degrade public health infrastructure. The chance of a serious global health crisis between now and 2030 is genuinely high, and it would scramble all the geopolitics by either forcing cooperation or, more likely, accelerating the fragmentation Jiang describes via different mechanisms.

A successful Chinese reunification gambit during the Iran distraction. The textbook "U.S. is bogged down, China moves on Taiwan" scenario is what every analyst red-teams. The curveball version: it works, not through invasion but through a quarantine-and-negotiate approach that splits the U.S. from its Pacific allies. China learns from Russia's Ukraine mistakes, picks the moment when American carriers are committed to Hormuz, and presents a fait accompli that the U.S. can't unwind without a war it can't politically stomach. Probability in next 3 years: maybe 15-20%, but if it happens it's the biggest reshape of the century.

Trump dies or is incapacitated. He's 79. Actuarially, the probability he doesn't finish his current term is non-trivial (somewhere in the 15-25% range across actuarial tables for a man his age and risk profile). What happens then depends entirely on Vance, the courts, and the GOP — all wild cards. This isn't dramatic, but it's the highest-probability "curveball" in the entire scenario space, and Jiang's "third-term" prediction completely ignores it.

The boring curveball: nothing dramatic happens. This is the curveball nobody discusses. It's possible that the Iran war becomes another grinding, inconclusive Middle East conflict like Iraq or Syria — bad, expensive, demoralizing, but not regime-changing for the U.S. or for the global order. The dollar stays dominant, NATO stays largely intact, China continues its slow march, and in 2030 we look back and say "well, that was bad, but it wasn't the end of anything." Doomsayers and revolutionaries both lose money on this scenario, which is why it's unfashionable, but historically it's been the modal outcome of "this changes everything" predictions.
